A The Calculus Behind The Notation

The main chapters use only the step equation and a few results from it. This appendix explains the calculus notation \(dX = v\,dt + \sigma\,dW\) and where the density formula of Chapter 4 comes from. You do not need it to use the model.

A.1 Slopes And Sums

Without noise, the evidence would grow smoothly at rate \(v\):

\[ \frac{dX}{dt} = v. \]

This is a derivative: a slope, the limit of \(\Delta X / \Delta t\) as the interval \(\Delta t\) shrinks to zero. "The slope of \(X\) over time is \(v\)."

Integration goes the other way: adding up all the tiny changes gives the total,

\[ X(t) = X(0) + \int_0^t v\,ds = X(0) + v\,t. \]

With \(v = 1\) and a start of 0.6, after 0.3 s the evidence is \(0.6 + 0.3 = 0.9\). Integrating means adding up thin slices: 300 slices of 1 ms, each adding 0.001.

A.2 Adding Noise: Sums Still Work, Slopes Do Not

With noise, add up the step equation over \(T\) seconds, made of \(n = T / \Delta t\) steps:

\[ X(T) = X(0) + \sum_{k=1}^{n} v\,\Delta t + \sum_{k=1}^{n} \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\varepsilon_k. \]

  • The drift sum is exactly as before: \(n \times v\,\Delta t = v\,T\).
  • The noise sum adds \(n\) independent normal draws, each with variance \(\sigma^2 \Delta t\). Their total is normal with variance \(n \sigma^2 \Delta t = \sigma^2 T\).

So, ignoring the boundaries, the evidence at time \(T\) is normally distributed with mean \(X(0) + v\,T\) and SD \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\), whatever the step size. This adding-up of random changes is a stochastic integral, and the noise part added up from 0 to \(t\) is the Wiener process \(W(t)\), scaled by \(\sigma\).

The slope is where it breaks. The noise part's change over one step, divided by the step length, is

\[ \frac{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\varepsilon}{\Delta t} = \frac{\sigma\,\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \]

which grows without limit as \(\Delta t\) shrinks: its SD is about 32 per second with 1 ms steps and 1,000 with 1 µs steps. The path never settles into a slope, however far you zoom in. That is why the model is written as \(dX = v\,dt + \sigma\,dW\), which only talks about changes over tiny intervals, and never as \(dX/dt = \dots\). Equations of this kind are called stochastic differential equations.

A.3 From Paths To Probability: The Diffusion Equation

To find when \(X\) first reaches a boundary, it helps to stop following single paths and follow the probability over all paths instead.

Picture a drop of ink in a channel of flowing water. Without boundaries, the probability of where \(X\) could be at time \(t\) is a normal bump: it starts as a spike at \(z a\), its centre drifts at speed \(v\), and it spreads out with SD \(\sigma\sqrt{t}\). This spreading and drifting obeys a partial differential equation, the diffusion (or Fokker-Planck) equation:

\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = -v\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\,\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial x^2}, \]

where \(p(x, t)\) is the probability density of the evidence being at level \(x\) at time \(t\). The first term shifts probability along with the drift. The second spreads it out: it is the heat equation from physics, and noise smooths out probability in the same way heat smooths out temperature.

A.4 Absorbing Boundaries And The Density

The boundaries act like absorbent walls: probability that reaches 0 or \(a\) is removed, because those paths have made their response. Over time the probability left between the walls shrinks. The rate at which it leaks out through the upper wall at time \(t\) is the upper-boundary density \(f_{\text{upper}}(t)\), and likewise for the lower wall. These are the curves of Chapter 4, and the limits that the histograms of simulated paths approach.

Solving the heat equation between two absorbing walls is a classic problem with two standard solutions, which are the two series in Chapter 4:

  • the large-time series builds the solution from sine waves that fit between the walls, each dying away at its own rate. That is where the \(\sin(k\pi z)\) and \(\exp(-k^2\pi^2 u / 2)\) terms come from. After a while only the slowest wave is left, so few terms are needed at large times;
  • the small-time series places mirror-image normal bumps on the far side of each wall (the "method of images") so that the total is zero at the walls. That is where the \((z + 2k)\) terms come from, one per mirror position. At small times the probability has not spread far, so only the nearest mirrors matter.

The factor \(\exp(-v a z - v^2 t / 2)\) in front adjusts the solution without drift for the drift.

A.5 Summary

Level What it describes
Step equation what happens on one trial, as tiny additions
Stochastic integral where one path is at any time
Diffusion equation where all paths are: a spreading, drifting distribution
Absorbing walls the rate of leaking through each wall is the RT density for each response
The two series exact solutions for that leaking rate, used in fitting
